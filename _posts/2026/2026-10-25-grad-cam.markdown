Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) is a technique for understanding **which spatial regions** of an image **contribute to a neural network's classification**. Suppose we train a neural network to classify images as either **cats or dogs**. When the model predicts "cat," we want to answer:

- Which parts of the image provide evidence for "cat"?

- Which parts provide evidence against "cat"?

- How do the network's activations and gradients combine to produce a heatmap?

In essence, Grad-CAM is a first-order estimate of contribution relative to zero features / inputs through Taylor Expansion.

To understand Grad-CAM, we'll build a tiny neural network and calculate everything by hand.

## 1. Our Tiny Neural Network

The architecture is:

```
RGB Image (3 × 2 × 2)
         |
         v
Global Average Pooling
         |
         v
RGB Averages (3 values)
         |
         v
Fully Connected (3 → 2)
         |
         v
       ReLU
         |
         v
      Softmax
         |
         v
   P(cat), P(dog)
```

Global average pooling is not a trainable layer. The fully connected layer contains the network's only learnable parameters.

### Input image

We use a very small image with just four RGB pixels:

|Position|Red|Green|Blue|
|---|---|---|---|
|Top-left|2|0|0|
|Top-right|1|0|0|
|Bottom-left|0|0|2|
|Bottom-right|0|1|0|

We use simplified RGB values rather than the usual 0–255 or normalized 0–1 range.

We can represent the input as:

$$
X\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2\times2}
$$

The first dimension represents the three color channels. Each channel is a 2 × 2 grid laid out like the image (top row first):

$$
R=\begin{bmatrix}
2&1\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix},\quad
G=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
2&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 1: Global Average Pooling

Average each color channel over the four spatial positions.

For red:

$$
\bar R=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
2&1\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{2+1+0+0}{4}=0.75
$$

For green:

$$
\bar G=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{0+0+0+1}{4}=0.25
$$

For blue:

$$
\bar B=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
2&0
\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{0+0+2+0}{4}=0.5
$$

Therefore:

$$
\bar X=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.75\\
0.25\\
0.5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 2: Fully Connected Layer

The fully connected layer produces two outputs, one for cat and one for dog. We manually choose these weights: ($2\times3$ matrix)

$$
W=
\begin{bmatrix}
2&0&-1\\
-1&0&2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first row contains the cat weights, and the second contains the dog weights. We set biases to zero.

The output is:

$$
a=W\bar X
$$

For cat:

$$
a_{\text{cat}}=2(0.75)+0(0.25)-1(0.5)
$$

$$
\boxed{a_{\text{cat}}=1.0}
$$

For dog:

$$
a_{\text{dog}}=-1(0.75)+0(0.25)+2(0.5)
$$

$$
\boxed{a_{\text{dog}}=0.25}
$$

These invented weights make red favor cats and blue favor dogs. They are not learned from real images.

### Step 3: ReLU

ReLU removes negative values:

$$
\operatorname{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x)
$$

Our two values are positive, so neither changes:

$$
z_{\text{cat}}=1.0
$$

$$
z_{\text{dog}}=0.25
$$

We will use $z$ to denote the output scores before Softmax.

### Step 4: Softmax

Softmax converts our scores into class probabilities:

$$
P(\text{cat})=
\frac{e^{z_{\text{cat}}}}
{e^{z_{\text{cat}}}+e^{z_{\text{dog}}}}
$$

Substituting:

$$
P(\text{cat})=
\frac{e^{1.0}}{e^{1.0}+e^{0.25}}
$$

$$
\boxed{P(\text{cat})\approx0.679}
$$

Similarly:

$$
\boxed{P(\text{dog})\approx0.321}
$$

Our network predicts **cat, with approximately 67.9% probability**. Now we want to know which pixels contributed to that decision.

## 2. How Grad-CAM Works

Grad-CAM combines two things:

- **Activations:** The feature values present at different spatial locations.

- **Gradients:** How sensitive the target score is to changes in those activations.

Standard Grad-CAM operates on a convolutional feature map. In this toy example, we'll treat the spatial RGB input as our feature map:

$$
A=X\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2\times2}
$$

This is a simplified Grad-CAM-style calculation, not a conventional convolutional Grad-CAM implementation.

We'll initially target the cat score:

$$
S=z_{\text{cat}}
$$

Later, we'll use cat minus dog to explain the classification decision more directly.

### Step 1: Calculate the gradients

We want:

$$
g_{c,u,v}=
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
$$

Here:

- $c$: color channel, red, green, or blue.
- $(u,v)$: spatial position.
- $A_{c,u,v}$: activation at that channel and position.
- $S$: cat score before Softmax.

Consider the red channel. From the weight matrix, we know:

$$
S=2\bar R-\bar B
$$

And:

$$
R=\begin{bmatrix}
R_1&R_2\\
R_3&R_4
\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\bar R=\frac{R_1+R_2+R_3+R_4}{4}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial R_i} = 2\frac{\partial\bar R}{\partial R_i}
$$

Since:

$$
\frac{\partial\bar R}{\partial R}=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac14&\frac14\\
\frac14&\frac14
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Red gradients**

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial R}= G_R=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5&0.5\\
0.5&0.5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Green gradients**

$$
G_G=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Blue gradients**

$$
G_B=
\begin{bmatrix}
-0.25&-0.25\\
-0.25&-0.25
\end{bmatrix}
$$

A positive gradient means increasing that activation would increase the cat score locally. A negative gradient means increasing it would decrease the cat score.

### Step 2: Average gradients within each channel

Standard Grad-CAM computes one weight for each channel:

$$
\alpha_c=
\frac1{HW}
\sum_{u,v}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
$$

Here, $H=W=2$. For red:

$$
\alpha_R=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
0.5&0.5\\
0.5&0.5
\end{bmatrix}\right)=0.5
$$

For green:

$$
\alpha_G=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}\right)=0
$$

For blue:

$$
\alpha_B=\operatorname{mean}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
-0.25&-0.25\\
-0.25&-0.25
\end{bmatrix}\right)=-0.25
$$

Our three Grad-CAM channel weights are:

$$
\boxed{
\alpha=
\begin{bmatrix}
0.5\\
0\\
-0.25
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

Notice that the gradient weights are different from the original fully connected weights because of the global average pooling operation.

### Step 3: Multiply each channel's activations by its Grad-CAM weight

The signed Grad-CAM heatmap is:

$$
M(u,v)=\sum_c\alpha_c A_c(u,v)
$$

For our three color channels:

$$
M(u,v)=0.5R(u,v)+0G(u,v)-0.25B(u,v)
$$


In matrix form:

$$
M=0.5\begin{bmatrix}
2&1\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}+0\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix}-0.25\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
2&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Our final signed heatmap is:

$$
\boxed{
M=
\begin{bmatrix}
1.0&0.5\\
-0.5&0
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

### Step 4: Interpret the heatmap

|Pixel|Attribution|Interpretation|
|---|---|---|
|Top-left|+1.0|Strongest evidence favoring cat|
|Top-right|+0.5|Some evidence favoring cat|
|Bottom-left|−0.5|Evidence against cat|
|Bottom-right|0|No contribution through this path|

The top-left red pixel has the strongest positive attribution, while the blue pixel has negative attribution.

**The critical distinction:** This heatmap is not just a sensitivity map.

It combines **sensitivity and activation magnitude**. A pixel can have a large gradient but zero activation, producing zero Gradient × Activation attribution.

Also, conventional Grad-CAM applies ReLU after combining the channels:

$$
M_{\text{Grad-CAM}}=\operatorname{ReLU}(M)
$$

So the conventional heatmap would be:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1.0&0.5\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This removes negative evidence. For our explanation, we'll keep the signed version so both supporting and opposing regions remain visible.

## 3. Explain Cat Versus Dog Instead of Just Cat

Our previous target was:

$$
S=z_{\text{cat}}
$$

But a classification depends on the relative scores of cat and dog.

A more informative target is:

$$
\boxed{S=z_{\text{cat}}-z_{\text{dog}}}
$$

Since:

$$
z_{\text{cat}}=1.0,\quad z_{\text{dog}}=0.25
$$

we have:

$$
S=0.75
$$

A positive value means cat is favored over dog.

In this example, both ReLUs are active. Therefore:

$$
S=(2\bar R-\bar B)-(-\bar R+2\bar B)
$$

Simplifying:

$$
\boxed{S=3\bar R-3\bar B}
$$

The derivatives are now:

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial R_i}=\frac34=0.75
$$

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial B_i}=-\frac34=-0.75
$$

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial G_i}=0
$$

As 2 × 2 gradient maps:

$$
G_R=\begin{bmatrix}
0.75&0.75\\
0.75&0.75
\end{bmatrix},\quad
G_G=\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix},\quad
G_B=\begin{bmatrix}
-0.75&-0.75\\
-0.75&-0.75
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The signed Grad-CAM heatmap becomes:

$$
M(u,v)=0.75R(u,v)-0.75B(u,v)
$$

Calculating each pixel:

$$
M=0.75\begin{bmatrix}
2&1\\
0&0
\end{bmatrix}-0.75\begin{bmatrix}
0&0\\
2&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\boxed{
M=
\begin{bmatrix}
1.5&0.75\\
-1.5&0
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

This time:

- Positive values provide evidence favoring cat over dog.

- Negative values provide evidence favoring dog over cat.

- Zero means no contribution to the margin in this simplified model.

Notice that:

$$
\sum_{u,v}M(u,v)=\operatorname{sum}\left(\begin{bmatrix}
1.5&0.75\\
-1.5&0
\end{bmatrix}\right)=1.5+0.75-1.5+0
$$

$$
\boxed{\sum_{u,v}M(u,v)=0.75=S}
$$

In this particular network, the sum of the signed heatmap exactly reproduces the cat-minus-dog score.

This happens because the network uses global average pooling, has zero biases, and both ReLU outputs are in their active linear regions.

It is not a general property of all Grad-CAM maps.

## 4. Grad-CAM Versus Gradient × Activation

So far, we calculated:

$$
\alpha_c=
\frac1{HW}\sum_{u,v}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
$$

Then:

$$
M(u,v)=\sum_c\alpha_c A_{c,u,v}
$$

But averaging gradients can lose spatial information.

Another method is **Gradient × Activation**:

$$
\boxed{
G(u,v)=
\sum_c
A_{c,u,v}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
}
$$

The difference is that Gradient × Activation preserves the gradient at each spatial position.

For our toy network, the gradients are constant at every position within each channel because the fully connected layer operates on global averages.

Therefore:

$$
G(u,v)=M(u,v)
$$

They produce identical heatmaps.

In a deeper CNN, however, gradients often vary spatially. Then Grad-CAM and Gradient × Activation may produce different results.

This is particularly relevant when analyzing earlier convolutional layers.

## 5. What Happens in a Real CNN?

A real cat-and-dog classifier typically contains multiple convolutional layers:

```
RGB Image
    |
    v
Convolution 1
    |
    v
   ReLU
    |
    v
Convolution 2
    |
    v
   ReLU
    |
    v
Convolution 3
    |
    v
Feature Maps (64 × 12 × 12)
    |
    v
Global Average Pooling
    |
    v
Fully Connected Layers
    |
    v
Cat / Dog Logits
    |
    v
Softmax
```

Instead of using the raw RGB values as activations, standard Grad-CAM uses intermediate convolutional feature maps.

For example:

$$
A\in\mathbb{R}^{64\times12\times12}
$$

There are now 64 feature channels rather than three RGB channels.

One channel might respond to edges, another to textures, and others to more complex learned patterns. Their interpretations are not guaranteed to correspond neatly to human concepts.

For each channel, Grad-CAM calculates:

$$
\alpha_c=
\frac1{144}
\sum_{u,v}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
$$

Then combines all 64 channels:

$$
M(u,v)=\sum_{c=1}^{64}\alpha_c A_{c,u,v}
$$

This produces a 12 × 12 attribution map.

The map can be upsampled to the original image resolution and overlaid on the image.

However, an upsampled heatmap does not have the spatial precision of the original pixels. A feature-map cell can depend on a large receptive field of the original image.

---

## 6. What Grad-CAM Can and Cannot Tell Us

Grad-CAM can show which feature-map locations provide positive or negative evidence for a chosen score.

But there are important limitations.

**First, attribution is not the same as sensitivity.**

The gradient alone measures local sensitivity:

$$
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}
$$

Grad-CAM multiplies activations by channel-averaged gradients, so its output reflects both feature presence and score sensitivity.

**Second, Grad-CAM does not prove causation.**

A high attribution value suggests that a region contributes to the current prediction. It does not guarantee that deleting that region would change the score by exactly that amount.

Removing pixels can change many downstream activations and even the gradients themselves.

**Third, the target score matters.**

Using:

$$
S=z_{\text{cat}}
$$

asks which regions support the cat score.

Using:

$$
S=z_{\text{cat}}-z_{\text{dog}}
$$

asks which regions favor cat relative to dog.

These can produce different heatmaps.

**Fourth, Softmax is generally not the best target for attribution.**

Although the network outputs probabilities, Grad-CAM is commonly computed using a pre-Softmax class score. Softmax couples the classes and can produce small gradients when probabilities saturate.

In our toy network, we therefore use the pre-Softmax score or logit margin.

## Conclusion

Grad-CAM uses gradients to determine the importance of feature channels, then weights their spatial activations to produce a heatmap.

The essential mathematics is:

$$
\boxed{
\alpha_c=
\frac1{HW}
\sum_{u,v}
\frac{\partial S}{\partial A_{c,u,v}}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
M(u,v)=\sum_c\alpha_c A_{c,u,v}
}
$$

The result highlights where the model has learned features that support or oppose a particular score.

For a real CNN, the most useful interpretation is:

**Grad-CAM shows where influential learned features are activated, not a direct measurement of how much each original image pixel would change the prediction if edited.**

To test whether those highlighted regions genuinely control the prediction, we can follow up with perturbation-based experiments, such as deleting or replacing selected image regions and measuring the resulting score change.
